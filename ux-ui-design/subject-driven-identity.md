# Subject-Driven Visual Identity

The fastest way to make something look generated rather than designed is to pick a layout template first and fill it with whatever content and colors the project happens to have. The alternative — used throughout this entry — is to start from the subject itself: what is this specific thing, what's its material world, its vernacular, its occasion, and what palette and type would someone who actually cared about *this* pick, not what would work for any similar-shaped project.

**See it applied:** [Theme Specimens](https://claude.ai/code/artifact/0288efad-ff46-4f4d-a8b0-429c46174eae) — 12 concrete subjects (a birthday party, a gallery show, a Halloween block party, a formal gala, a coffee roastery, two Substack newsletters, a winter open house, a wedding, a music festival, a farmers market, and a bookshop staff pick), each given its own palette, type pairing, and one structural detail specific to what it actually is — not the same card with the accent color changed.

This is [avoiding-the-ai-generated-look.md](avoiding-the-ai-generated-look.md) from the other direction: that entry names the tells of a templated design; this one is the practice that avoids producing them in the first place.

## Where each identity actually comes from

| # | Theme | Where the identity comes from |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Birthday | Playful, rounded, hand-tilted — a party invite pinned slightly crooked to a fridge, not a corporate announcement. Confetti as scattered dots, not an emoji. |
| 2 | Photo Gallery | A gallery wall, not a photo app: near-black ground, small-caps museum-label type, a contact-sheet strip standing in for the photographs themselves. |
| 3 | Halloween | A torn poster, not a plastic pumpkin — deep aubergine instead of the cliché near-black-plus-acid-green combo, a jagged clipped edge, a wanted-poster display face. |
| 4 | Official Event | Symmetric and centered *on purpose* — the one theme where centering is the subject-correct choice, not a lazy default, because formal invitations are actually set this way. |
| 5 | Winter Holiday | Desaturated pine and cranberry instead of stock red-and-green, a sprig suggested by three tick marks on a rule rather than a literal icon. |
| 6 | Wedding | A gold hairline frame, an ampersand doing the emotional work typographically instead of a script font doing it decoratively. |
| 7 | Music Festival | Poster-condensed display type, two saturated pops instead of the single-accent-on-black cliché, a torn dashed edge like a ticket stub. |
| 8 | Coffee | Dark roast and a bold condensed sans instead of the warm-cream-serif-terracotta combination that's become the default "artisan" look — deliberately the opposite call. |
| 9 | Substack (Personal) | The most restrained tile on the page on purpose — an essay newsletter's own aesthetic *is* restraint; adding decoration here would work against the subject, not for it. |
| 10 | Substack — Nature | A field-guide specimen label: a dotted double-rule like a herbarium tag, a serif built for long reading, moss and berry instead of the personal edition's cooler blue. |
| 11 | Farmers Market | A stamped, slightly misaligned double border — the visual signature of a produce crate stencil, not a decorative rounded card. |
| 12 | Literary / Bookshop | Spine-stripes down one edge standing in for books shelved spine-out, a numbered catalog entry because a staff-pick series genuinely is a numbered sequence. |

## The practice, generalized

**Ask what's different about this subject's world before choosing anything.** A coffee roastery, a wedding, and a bookshop could all technically use a warm neutral palette with a serif headline — which is exactly why that combination shows up everywhere and reads as generic the moment it does. The useful question isn't "what looks good here" (most competent choices look fine) — it's "what would someone who only knew how to design for *this specific thing* pick, that they wouldn't pick for something else."

**Let the palette come from material, not decoration.** The farmers-market tile's kraft-tan-and-tomato palette comes from produce crates and stall signage; the coffee tile's palette comes from roast levels, not from "colors that feel warm and cozy." A palette grounded in something real in the subject's world is harder to accidentally reuse on an unrelated project than one chosen for abstract mood.

**Give each identity exactly one structural signature, not a pile of decoration.** Every tile above gets one specific device — a torn edge, a stamped double-border, a spine-stripe, a centered symmetric frame — rather than several competing motifs. One well-chosen structural detail reads as intentional; three or four read as decoration for its own sake, which is one of the tells in [avoiding-the-ai-generated-look.md](avoiding-the-ai-generated-look.md).

**Type pairing is where the identity is most often skipped.** It's easy to spend real effort on a distinct palette and motif and then default to whatever sans-serif was already loaded. Two different subjects that share a headline typeface but nothing else will still read as more similar than two subjects that share a palette but use genuinely different type — type carries personality even when nothing else about the page changes.

**Restraint is a valid identity, not an absence of one.** The Substack (Personal) tile is deliberately the least decorated tile in the set — for a personal essay newsletter, visual quiet *is* the correct, subject-specific choice, the same way loud, condensed, poster type is correct for a festival. Treating "minimal" as the default for every subject that doesn't have an obvious motif is the mistake; treating it as *the specific right call for this specific subject* is the practice.
